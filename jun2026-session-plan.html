Why dialogue is a good fit for Marcello

His oral storytelling is one of his strongest skills; his bottleneck is compressing spoken language into written sentence structure. Dialogue lets him put a voice he can already HEAR onto the page — inside a fixed, scaffoldable shape (who + said + "the words").

The dialogue builder externalizes that structure so the only generation load is the words themselves. The quotation-mark "hug" is the one new concept — keep it concrete.

Watch-for

The new concept is that quotation marks hug ONLY the spoken words. Watch for him quoting the whole sentence, or dropping the marks. Use the "hug" metaphor + read it aloud.

Don't skip the say-aloud step on the dialogue sentence — that's the lever. Build → say the whole thing out loud → then check & lock.

Part A word-traps: "red" (A), "knitting" (C), "unfamiliar" (D) all reuse a story word. Slow him on those — matching a word ≠ the message.

Reflexive "yep that matches" on the source-check → verbal mirror: read his sentence aloud, read the panel label aloud, "same thing?"

Session Plan (50 min)

3 min

Phase 1 — Greeting + frame

Normal energy, home stretch of the year. Brief check-in — quick, not a quiz.

Frame: "One short story today — about a red scarf a family passes down. We'll make it a 4-panel comic, answer one big question, then write two sentences. The second one is a NEW kind — a TALKING sentence."

Connection: "Your class is reading Grandfather's Journey and The Keeping Quilt — families carrying things across the world. And they're working on dialogue, characters TALKING. So are we today."

No Phase 0 recovery — keep the 4 moves in reserve only if he arrives flat.

18 min

Phase 2 — Tab 1: Read & Draw "The Red Scarf" 🧣

One paragraph at a time. Four paragraphs, ~4–5 min each.

Read (pick reader early, stick with it) → Say it in own words (the active-paraphrase pause — do NOT let this slide) → Draw the panel (320×220, stick figures) → 1–2 word label → tap "I read it".

Speech bubbles! On panels 1 & 3 (where characters talk), have him draw little speech bubbles for the talking parts. Point to the highlighted quoted lines. This is the visual bridge into the dialogue sentence in Phase 4.

If "unfamiliar" trips him on paragraph 2: whole word → point at un- → familiar. One quick pass, no drill.

Panel matches the wrong gist? Don't correct — "show me the part of the paragraph this panel is about." Let the gap surface in his re-read.

The 4 paragraphs (rough):
  1. Grandmother in a cold country knits a red scarf for her boy (father). "Wear it every winter," she said. "It will keep you close to me."
  2. Father, grown, leaves the old country, flies across the ocean. The new land is unfamiliar. He wears the scarf; it helps him feel brave.
  3. Father grows up, marries, has the narrator. On the narrator's 7th birthday he wraps the scarf around their neck. "Now it is yours," he said. I asked, "Will it keep me warm too?" "Yes," he said. "And it will keep you close to us."
  4. The scarf is older than the narrator, older than father. Every winter they wear it and think of grandmother knitting. "It keeps me warm, and it keeps my whole family close."
9 min

Phase 3 — Tab 2: The Big Question with Y/N/M 🤔

One EBSR pair (central message, RL.3.2). Walk Y/N/M explicitly — narrate each rating before he taps. Yes = could be it; No = sure it's wrong; Maybe = not sure.

Part A — "What is the central message of 'The Red Scarf'?"

  • A. Red is a good color for a scarf. (word-trap — "red"/"scarf")
  • B. A family treasure keeps the people we love close. ✓ correct
  • C. Knitting keeps you warm in the winter. (word-trap — "knitting"/"warm")
  • D. New places can feel unfamiliar. (word-trap — "unfamiliar" is a detail)

Part B — "Which sentence BEST shows the central message?" (unlocks on A correct)

  • A. "She made it for her little boy — my father — to keep him warm." (how/why made)
  • B. "Everything was unfamiliar — the streets, the food, even the snow." (voyage difficulty)
  • C. "It keeps me warm, and it keeps my whole family close." ✓ correct (shows the message)
  • D. "He got married, and then I was born." (timeline)

Three traps in Part A all reuse a story word. If he Yes-rates one because the word feels familiar: "is the STORY about how nice the color red is — or about a thing a family passes down to keep everyone close?"

16 min

Phase 4 — Tab 3: Write 2 sentences — incl. a TALKING sentence 🗣️

Sentence 1 — narrative (warm-up, known skill). Anchor to any panel → say what happens → write → source-check against the panel → lock.

Sentence 2 — DIALOGUE (the new work). Anchor to Panel 3 (the talking panel).

  1. Who is talking in this panel? → Grandmother / Father / I
  2. What did they say? → type the exact words
  3. Tell-word → said / asked / answered
  4. The preview assembles it: Father said, "Now it is yours." Tap "Put it in the box".
  5. Say the whole sentence out loud (act it if he wants) — the lever.
  6. Source-check: (1) matches the panel? (2) do the quotes hug ONLY the spoken words? → lock.

Key script: "Who's talking in this panel? What are the EXACT words coming out of their mouth? The quotation marks are a hug around just those words. Now say the whole thing out loud."

The teaching point (don't over-explain): quotation marks go around the exact words; the tell-word (said/asked) tells who is talking. That's dialogue.

If time runs short: the dialogue sentence is the priority. ONE locked talking sentence with a real check > two rushed ones. If you only finish one, finish Sentence 2.

4 min

Phase 5 — Stars + preview ⭐

Tap stars on sentences he's proud of. No "Wish" today.

"You read it, drew it, answered the big question — and you made a character TALK with dialogue. That's exactly what your class is doing with Grandfather's Journey and The Keeping Quilt this week."

Preview: "Next time we'll do more talking — maybe two characters going back and forth." (1–2 sessions left; final one = a summer keep-writing kit.)
